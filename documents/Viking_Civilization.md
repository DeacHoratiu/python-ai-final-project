# The History of the Vikings

*A history of Scandinavia and the Viking Age, approximately 750–1100 CE*

## 1. Scandinavia Before the Viking Age

The people now remembered as Vikings came from Scandinavia, especially the regions that became Denmark, Norway, and Sweden. Their homeland was not a single kingdom or a united nation. It contained farming districts, coastal communities, trading places, and territories governed by local leaders. Mountains, forests, rivers, islands, and long coastlines shaped how people lived. The sea did not simply divide Scandinavian settlements; it also connected them. Travel by water could be more practical than crossing difficult country on foot.

Long before the famous raids of the late eighth century, Scandinavian communities had maintained contact with the rest of Europe. Archaeological discoveries show imported objects, elaborate metalwork, and changing patterns of wealth. During the centuries after the fall of the Western Roman Empire, local elites competed for followers and resources. Magnificent halls, richly furnished graves, and precious ornaments reveal that political and social differences were already well established.

Most people were not warriors traveling abroad. They lived on farms, kept animals, cultivated crops, cut timber, worked iron, and fished. Families depended on seasonal labor and cooperation within their communities. The growing importance of ships, craft specialization, and overseas exchange prepared the conditions for the expansion that historians call the Viking Age.

The word *Viking* requires some care. In medieval Scandinavian usage, words related to *víking* could describe an expedition overseas, particularly one involving raiding, rather than every inhabitant of Scandinavia. Modern historians use *Viking Age* for a wider period of Scandinavian travel, warfare, migration, settlement, and commerce. The people themselves belonged to varied societies and pursued many different occupations.

## 2. The Opening of the Viking Age

The raid on the monastery of Lindisfarne, off the northeastern English coast, in 793 CE is a traditional starting point for the Viking Age. Christian writers were shocked by the attack on a religious community. It was not the first encounter between Scandinavians and the British Isles, and an earlier recorded incident occurred at Portland in southern England in 789. Nevertheless, Lindisfarne became a powerful symbol of a new and frightening pattern of seaborne violence.

Monasteries could be tempting targets. They sometimes possessed valuable religious objects and stored resources, while their defenses were limited. Raiding parties used their mobility to strike at places where defenders were unprepared and then escape by sea. Attacks also reached Ireland, Scotland, the Frankish lands, and other coasts. The community at Iona, off western Scotland, suffered repeated attacks early in the ninth century.

Contemporary accounts often described the attackers as heathens or foreigners. Such sources preserve important evidence, but they were usually written by victims or their neighbors and naturally emphasized devastation. Archaeology adds a broader view: Scandinavian travelers were also merchants, craftsmen, settlers, and political allies. Warfare and trade could even be undertaken by the same individuals at different times.

There was no single cause of Viking expansion. Possible factors include competition between Scandinavian rulers, the pursuit of portable wealth, opportunities for trade, access to new farmland, and improvements in shipbuilding and navigation. Pressure on land and population may have mattered in some places, but it cannot explain every expedition. As information about distant opportunities spread, overseas activity developed its own momentum.

## 3. Ships, Navigation, and the North Atlantic

Ships were essential to Scandinavian expansion. Builders made vessels from overlapping wooden planks, a method called clinker construction. These hulls combined flexibility with strength and could travel through rough seas. Many warships were long, relatively narrow, and shallow-drafted. They could be rowed or sailed and could approach beaches and rivers that larger vessels found difficult to enter.

Not every Viking ship was a longship. Cargo vessels, sometimes known as *knarrs*, were broader and designed to carry goods, animals, and supplies across open water. Archaeological finds, including ships preserved at Oseberg and Gokstad in Norway and vessels found at Roskilde in Denmark, reveal considerable variety in size and purpose. Successful voyages depended on skilled crews, maintenance, suitable weather, and careful planning rather than a single miraculous technology.

Sailors knew coastal landmarks, currents, winds, and the behavior of the sea. In the North Atlantic they also depended on experience, observation, and knowledge passed between generations. Stories about special navigational devices are popular, but firm evidence for some proposed methods, such as the routine Viking use of a “sunstone,” is limited. It is safer to recognize their practical maritime expertise than to attribute their success to an unproven invention.

Sea travel made rapid raids possible, but its historical importance was greater than warfare. Ships linked farms to markets and connected the North Sea with the Baltic, the Irish Sea, and eventually the North Atlantic islands. A person setting out as a trader might return with silver, textiles, information about foreign politics, and news of places where families could settle.

## 4. From Raids to Conquest in Britain and Ireland

During the ninth century, Scandinavian expeditions grew larger and their goals changed. Some groups established winter camps rather than returning home after each season. In 865, an army traditionally called the Great Heathen Army arrived in England. Over the following years its leaders fought several Anglo-Saxon kingdoms and gained control over extensive territories.

Northumbria, East Anglia, and Mercia experienced major changes. Wessex remained a center of resistance. King Alfred of Wessex defeated a Viking army at Edington in 878 and negotiated a settlement with its leader, Guthrum. The resulting political geography is often discussed under the term *Danelaw*, a name associated with areas where Danish influence and legal traditions became prominent. The boundaries and institutions of this region changed over time; it was never simply one permanent Viking state.

Scandinavian settlers established homes, cultivated land, and participated in local markets. Old Norse influenced place names and vocabulary in parts of England. York, known in Old Norse as Jórvík, became an important commercial and political center. The remains of streets, workshops, and everyday objects help historians understand its population beyond the stories of battles and kings.

Across the Irish Sea, Scandinavian raiders and settlers helped develop coastal bases that grew into towns. Dublin became especially significant as a center of trade and political power. Scandinavian leaders also formed alliances and fought with Irish rulers, sometimes against other Scandinavians. The history of Ireland during this period cannot be reduced to a simple contest between two separate peoples: loyalties shifted, marriages connected families, and new communities emerged.

Viking attacks also struck the Carolingian world. Fleets traveled up rivers such as the Seine and Loire. In 911, the Frankish ruler Charles the Simple reached an agreement with the Scandinavian leader Rollo, traditionally associated with the beginnings of Normandy. Over generations, Scandinavian settlers there adopted the local language and Christianity while contributing to a new regional ruling class.

## 5. Trade Routes Across Europe and Asia

The Viking world extended far beyond the coasts attacked by raiders. Scandinavian merchants traveled through the Baltic Sea and into the great rivers of eastern Europe. Routes along the Volga and the Dnieper connected northern markets with the Black Sea, the Byzantine Empire, and regions of the Islamic world. Goods, coins, people, and stories moved along these networks through numerous intermediaries.

Trading places in Scandinavia included Ribe and Hedeby in the Danish sphere, Birka in Sweden, and Kaupang in Norway. They attracted craftspeople, buyers, and travelers. Excavations have uncovered scales, weights, jewelry-making equipment, imported glass, and silver coins. Large numbers of Islamic silver dirhams found in northern Europe illustrate the breadth of long-distance commercial exchange.

Scandinavian traders exported or exchanged materials such as furs, amber, timber, iron products, and walrus ivory. They obtained silver, silk, glassware, wine, and other desirable goods. Enslaved people were also traded. The slave trade was a serious and violent part of the economy, not a minor detail to be overlooked in accounts focused on exploration and craftsmanship.

In eastern Europe, people identified in sources as *Rus* were involved in trade and the formation of political networks. Scandinavian participation in the early history of the Rus is well attested, but the origins and development of Rus society involved Slavic, Finnic, Scandinavian, steppe, and Byzantine influences. Reducing the emergence of eastern European states to the actions of a single ethnic group would misrepresent a complex process.

Some Scandinavians entered Byzantine military service. The Varangian Guard, associated with the emperors of Constantinople, became famous for employing warriors from northern and later other European backgrounds. These eastern connections reveal a side of the Viking Age very different from the familiar image of ships attacking English monasteries.

## 6. Iceland, Greenland, and North America

Migration across the North Atlantic was one of the most remarkable developments of the Viking Age. Norse settlers reached the Faroe Islands and Iceland, where communities grew during the ninth and tenth centuries. Traditional accounts place Iceland's settlement beginning around 870, although archaeological evidence and later written narratives do not always provide identical dates.

Iceland developed a distinctive political system without a single king. Regional leaders known as *goðar* held influence, while an assembly called the Althing was established traditionally in 930. The Althing was an important meeting place for law and dispute settlement, but it should not be imagined as a modern democratic parliament with equal political power for everyone. Social standing, property, alliances, and the authority of powerful families strongly affected outcomes.

Around the end of the tenth century, Norse settlers led by Erik the Red established communities in Greenland. Their farms were concentrated in relatively sheltered coastal areas where livestock could be raised and some crops grown. Greenlandic settlers also hunted seals and obtained valuable Arctic products, including walrus ivory. Maintaining contact with Iceland and Norway was difficult, and environmental and economic circumstances changed over the centuries. Norse communities in Greenland survived long after the conventional end of the Viking Age before disappearing from the surviving record in the late medieval period.

Norse voyagers also reached North America. Icelandic sagas describe journeys associated with Leif Erikson and a region called Vinland. The archaeological site at L'Anse aux Meadows, in Newfoundland, Canada, provides firm evidence of a Norse presence in North America around the year 1000. Archaeological discoveries confirm the broad reality of transatlantic exploration, but not every detail of the much later saga narratives.

The travelers encountered lands already inhabited by Indigenous peoples. Written Norse accounts describe meetings and conflict, but these accounts must be approached cautiously. The North American venture did not develop into a lasting large-scale Norse colony. Its significance lies in the extraordinary reach of northern seafaring and the evidence it provides for contact across the Atlantic centuries before Columbus.

## 7. Farmers, Families, and Social Hierarchy

The vast majority of people in Viking Age Scandinavia spent their lives farming rather than raiding. Households grew grain where climate and soil allowed, raised cattle, sheep, pigs, and goats, and relied on fishing, hunting, and gathering. Winter preparation was crucial. Food had to be stored, buildings repaired, and livestock supported during cold months.

A farm was a center of both economic and social life. Large halls were important places for hospitality, negotiation, feasting, and the display of power. At the same time, many families occupied much more modest buildings. Spinning, weaving, food preparation, metalwork, woodworking, and boat maintenance all required specialized knowledge. Children learned practical skills within households and communities.

Viking Age society was hierarchical. Kings and powerful regional leaders competed for allegiance, wealthy landowners controlled resources, and free people had varying degrees of independence. Enslaved people, often called *thralls* in Old Norse contexts, could be forced to perform labor and could be bought or sold. The legal rights and daily circumstances of women and men also varied according to wealth, status, family situation, and region.

Women could manage property and farms in some circumstances, especially during the absence of male relatives. Burial evidence and later legal texts suggest a range of responsibilities and social roles. However, it would be misleading to describe Viking society as either uniformly egalitarian or identical across Scandinavia. Exceptional burials cannot establish what was normal for everyone.

Political decisions and disputes could be discussed at assemblies called *things*. These gatherings dealt with legal cases, agreements, and matters of communal concern. Their procedures differed across regions and changed over time. Such assemblies show the importance of law and negotiation in societies that also accepted warfare and personal feuds.

## 8. Gods, Rituals, and the Coming of Christianity

Before the spread of Christianity, Scandinavian religious practices were diverse and centered on multiple gods and sacred powers. Odin was associated with wisdom, kingship, poetry, and war; Thor with thunder and protection; Freyr and Freyja with important aspects of prosperity and divine power. Religious traditions also included stories about giants, spirits, fate, and a world imagined through complex mythic relationships.

Our knowledge of Norse mythology comes from several kinds of evidence. Place names, images, burials, and runic inscriptions provide traces from the Viking Age itself. Much fuller stories were written down in medieval Iceland after Christianization, especially in works associated with the Poetic Edda and Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda. These texts preserve valuable material, but their late composition means they cannot simply be treated as direct recordings of unchanged pre-Christian beliefs.

Religious activity could take place at halls, sacred sites, and gatherings. Offerings and feasts connected religious practice with social and political relationships. Burial customs varied greatly. Some people were buried with objects, ships, or animals; others received simpler graves. Cremation and inhumation both occurred. No single burial pattern describes the whole Viking world.

Christianization proceeded unevenly. Missionaries, merchants, and political rulers helped establish Christianity in different areas at different times. Harald Bluetooth, king of Denmark in the tenth century, is remembered for proclaiming on the Jelling stone that he made the Danes Christian. The inscription is a statement of royal authority, not proof that everyone changed beliefs overnight. Norway and Sweden also experienced lengthy and sometimes contested transitions.

The adoption of Christianity connected Scandinavian rulers more closely with European systems of kingship, literacy, church organization, and diplomacy. Older practices did not instantly disappear. For generations, religious identities and customs overlapped, and political change often accompanied conversion.

## 9. Writing, Art, and Historical Memory

The runic alphabets were used in Scandinavia before and during the Viking Age. The Younger Futhark, with sixteen basic runes, became especially common during this period. Runes appeared on stones, wood, metal, and other materials. Inscriptions commemorated relatives, named owners or makers, and occasionally recorded journeys or events. They were not a replacement for every form of spoken communication, and most surviving examples are relatively brief.

Runestones are especially useful because some preserve the names, relationships, and aspirations of people otherwise absent from chronicles. The Jelling monuments in Denmark stand among the most famous examples, linking royal memory, political ambition, and religious transformation. Their inscriptions tell us how rulers wished to be remembered, which is not always identical to an impartial account of their achievements.

Artisans decorated objects with intricate animal forms, interlaced lines, and patterns that changed through time. Styles now called Borre, Jelling, Mammen, Ringerike, and Urnes are among the terms used to describe Viking Age and later Scandinavian artistic developments. Elaborate carving and metalwork show considerable skill, and imported objects demonstrate the influence of long-distance connections.

Poetry was a powerful medium for preserving reputation and recounting deeds. Skalds composed complex verse for rulers and other patrons. Stories about heroes, disputes, voyages, and families were passed down and eventually recorded in Icelandic sagas. Many sagas were written in the thirteenth century or later, often centuries after the events they describe. They are literary achievements and sources for medieval attitudes, but historians must test their historical claims against contemporary records and archaeology.

Modern understanding of the Vikings is shaped by both evidence and invention. The familiar image of horned helmets, for example, is not supported as standard Viking battle equipment. Popular depictions of endlessly violent, culturally isolated raiders also overlook the richness of Scandinavian farming, trade, craftsmanship, and political life.

## 10. Kings, Kingdoms, and the End of the Viking Age

As the Viking Age progressed, Scandinavian kings gained authority over larger territories, although the process was uneven and often contested. Political power still depended heavily on personal alliances, military success, control of resources, and support from local leaders. The emergence of Denmark, Norway, and Sweden as more recognizable kingdoms took generations rather than following a single plan.

In England, Scandinavian intervention continued long after the ninth-century settlements. Sweyn Forkbeard, king of Denmark, conquered England in 1013 but died the following year. His son Cnut became king of England in 1016 and later ruled Denmark and Norway as well. Cnut's dominions joined lands around the North Sea under one monarch, but they were not a permanent, centrally unified empire. After his death in 1035, political unity weakened.

A conventional ending for the Viking Age is 1066. In September of that year, the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada invaded England and was defeated and killed by King Harold Godwinson at the Battle of Stamford Bridge. Weeks later, Harold himself was defeated by William of Normandy at Hastings. William's ancestors had settled in Normandy generations earlier, but his conquest belonged to a transformed political world.

There was no moment when every Viking ship stopped sailing. Scandinavian maritime activity, commerce, migration, and warfare continued. What changed was the wider setting: stronger kingdoms emerged, Christianity became firmly established, and political and economic relationships were increasingly organized through institutions recognizable in medieval Europe. The term *Viking Age* marks a major historical transformation rather than the disappearance of a people.

The legacy of the Vikings survives in place names, languages, archaeological sites, literature, and the histories of many regions. Scandinavian settlers contributed to communities in Britain, Ireland, Normandy, Iceland, Greenland, and eastern Europe. Their voyages connected the Baltic and North Atlantic with the Mediterranean and lands far to the east. Their actions included violence and enslavement as well as exploration, trade, artistic innovation, and settlement.

To understand Viking history is therefore to move beyond the image of the warrior aboard a longship. It is the history of farmers and rulers, craftspeople and merchants, migrants and captives, poets and shipbuilders: people whose choices reshaped the medieval world.
